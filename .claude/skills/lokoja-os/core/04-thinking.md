# Clear thinking

Writing cannot fix a confused argument. This file is the thinking that comes before the prose, and the challenge that comes after it. It merges the house decision engines, the Executive Communication Advisor, the Seven Gates analytical spine and the analytical sequences of the house operating systems.

## 1. Define the decision

Before any draft, write two internal sentences:

1. What does the reader need to decide or understand after reading this?
2. What is our answer, in one sentence?

If either is unclear, the piece is not ready to draft. Then fix the reader (who, what they already know, what they will do next), the boundary (asset, company, period, sample), the deadline and who may see it.

## 2. Structure the argument

- **Answer first (pyramid).** Conclusion; two to four supporting reasons; evidence under each; background last. Every supporting point directly supports the conclusion.
- **MECE grouping.** Reasons do not overlap and together cover what the decision needs. When source material is not MECE, find the overlap and the omission before writing.
- **SCQA for openings of reports and decks.** Situation, complication, question, answer, in four sentences at most. In an essay, the narrative opening may come first, but the answer arrives early.
- **The CEO test.** After the first two sentences, does a senior reader know what is recommended, why it matters and what to do?

## 3. Follow the mechanism

- **Mechanism before verdict.** Explain what changed, through which channel, who captures the economics, who bears the cost, how long it lasts, what the price already assumes, and where the next constraint appears.
- **Constraint chain.** Name the binding limit, then the next one once it is removed. Nameplate is not throughput; a trial peak is not a sustained rate; a removed bottleneck is not a solved system.
- **Consequence chain.** If the thesis works, which constraint moves, who gains bargaining power, who pays, what new cost appears elsewhere, what the competitor or regulator does next. Show the chain; do not call it second-order thinking.
- **Separate the layers.** Asset, business, management, balance sheet, security. Or: plant, operation, organisation, contract, money. Or: data, method, finding, interpretation, claim. A magnificent asset can be a dreadful security.

## 4. Before drafting: the absurdity sweep

List everything in the material that is physically, commercially, legally, arithmetically or procedurally impossible, and put those items first. An oil rate above the disposal limit, equity priced below debt, a plateau no well set can deliver, a sample that cannot support the generalisation, a date already in the past counted as a commitment, totals that do not add. State them flatly and let the fact carry the weight.

## 5. Calibrated judgement

- **Base rates first.** What usually happens to projects, companies, policies or theses of this kind? Start there, then adjust for the evidence.
- **Expectations test.** What does the price, consensus or plan already assume? Which assumptions do the most work? What can go right without being priced, and what can go wrong that the plan treats as impossible?
- **Scenarios that differ in kind.** Bear, base and bull (or low, reference, high) must be economically different worlds, each with its own mechanism and the evidence that would make it more likely. Not 8, 10 and 12 per cent versions of one spreadsheet.
- **Falsification.** For every conclusion, name the evidence that would change it.
- **Confidence.** High (strong evidence, stable logic), Medium (reasonable evidence, missing inputs), Low (limited evidence, unstable assumptions), Speculative (useful idea, not yet reliable). State the basis.
- **Value of information.** Rank further work by how much decision-relevant uncertainty it removes for its cost and time.
- **Make the call.** After the evidence and its limits, decide: proceed, proceed with conditions, hold, redesign, reject; buy, accumulate, hold, watch, avoid; accept, revise, reject.

## 6. Challenge (internal; never named in public copy)

Run challenge on any deep-mode deliverable, preferably with separate agents that have not seen the drafting. Each gets the draft, the sources and one role brief, and returns findings with severity (Critical, Major, Minor, Observation), evidence and fix.

Tools:

- **Kill shot**: the single objection the most informed sceptic would raise first, and what it would take to answer it.
- **Steelman**: the strongest version of the view we reject, stated so its holder would sign it.
- **Pre-mortem**: assume the recommendation failed in 18 months (three months for operations); ten likely causes, early warnings, prevention, owner.
- **Assumption fragility**: list assumptions, rank by fragility and value impact, name the evidence that would break each.
- **Fresh-eyes read**: a reader who knows nothing of the drafting reads only for clarity and tells.
- **Quantitative QA**: a separate pass recalculates every material number from source.

Keep a challenge log (finding, severity, response, decision, closure). Where credible disagreement remains, state the majority view and the credible alternative with its consequence. Do not manufacture consensus.

In published copy the challenge is the contrarian case, challenge case, alternative hypothesis, thesis stress test, robustness test or falsification test. "Red team" is internal vocabulary and never appears in public or client copy.

## 7. Decision engines (quick forms)

- **Decision memo**: recommendation; why; key assumptions; risks; alternatives; next action; confidence.
- **Investment memo**: verdict; business quality; financial quality; valuation; expectations; risks; contrarian case; entry and walk-away price; horizon and review date; monitoring triggers.
- **Bid review**: objective; client requirement; technical compliance; commercial exposure; execution risk; missing inputs; clarifications; risk register; pricing posture; submission fixes.
- **Project gates**: numbered gates where a failed gate stops the work behind it; each with evidence required, owner, closing test and consequence of failure. Title and authority usually come first.
- **Options table**: the same criteria for every option (safety, operability, integrity, production, cost, schedule, constructability, execution risk, value), and a stated weighting if one is used.

## 8. Thinking traps to check for

Narrative over arithmetic. Anchoring on the first number seen. Survivorship (only the companies or wells that lasted are in the sample). Confusing announcement with completion, filing with approval, production with sales, sales with cash. Treating a model output as a measurement. Averaging two inconsistent sources. Mistaking a good company for a good price. Mistaking activity for progress. Excitement about a thesis overriding valuation discipline. Optimism in a financial model before execution risk is closed. Underpricing local execution risk (logistics, community, security, delay).

## 9. Where the thinking goes in the text

The reader should find a point of view, then the mechanism supporting it, then the numbers capable of killing it. Put the risk beside the claim it qualifies, not in a box at the end where it cannot qualify anything.
