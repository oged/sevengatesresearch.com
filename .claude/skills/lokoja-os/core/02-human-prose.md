# Human prose: the merged engine

This file replaces Stop Slop, Clear Technical Writing, Hemingway, Hemingway-Oil's AI-tell section, the Seven Gates sections 5.5 and 5.6, and the voice sections of the house operating systems. Every room uses it. Rooms differ only in how much figurative language and humour they allow (section 7).

## 1. The principle

Write for the reader's working memory and the reader's intelligence at the same time. A sentence should be understood once, at reading speed, and should leave the reader knowing something they did not know before it.

Two failures look opposite and come from the same place:

- **Compression.** Dropped small words, noun stacks, coined pairs, fragments strung with semicolons. The reader has to rebuild the sentence. This is what post-training for dense output produces (Clark, Martin and Mahowald, arXiv:2610.29162).
- **Performance.** Signposts, verdicts, triads, slogans, pull-quote endings, borrowed profundity. The sentence advertises the writer instead of moving the argument.

Do not write to beat AI detectors. Do not add randomness, slang, deliberate errors or casual asides to "sound human". Substance does that job: real constraints, named actors, dated numbers, a mechanism, a decision.

## 2. Seven syntax checks (sentence level)

Run every sentence of prose through these. Titles, labels and table headers may use fragments, but checks 3, 4 and 6 still apply to them.

1. **Keep "that" and "which"** in relative and complement clauses longer than a few words. "The wells that we shut in last week need retesting." "Operations confirmed that the valve was closed."
2. **Nouns stay nouns.** Not "action the scope", "calendar it", "baseline the model", "ticket the defect". Established trade verbs are fine: pig a line, choke back a well, flare gas, short a stock.
3. **Inanimate subjects get literal verbs.** Data show; the plan assumes; the pump delivers; the valve isolates. Data do not tell, want, decide or hope. Markets do not know, notice, sulk or send invoices. A report states; it does not argue.
4. **Break noun stacks.** No more than three nouns in a row unless the chain is a fixed name (produced water, root cause, flowline service test). "The root cause review of the disposal pump trip at Station A", not "the Station A disposal pump trip root cause review".
5. **Keep related words close.** Subject and verb in the first few words. Long lists and modifiers at the end. One main point per sentence. Ceiling 25 words for description and 20 for instructions; most sentences shorter. List items share one grammatical form.
6. **Use word pairs a reader has seen.** Not "loss posture", "capacity runway", "recovery cadence", "reader-patience budget". Write "current losses", "remaining capacity", "recovery rate". Never repeat a coined phrase to make it look like a term.
7. **Keep the small words** (the, of, is, was, that) in anything written as a sentence. Fragments belong in tables and labels only.

## 3. Lexicon: delete on sight

Throat-clearing and signposts: it is important to note; it should be noted; it is worth noting; here's; let's; let me; here is the thing; let us unpack; importantly; notably; interestingly; essentially; basically; overall; in summary; to summarise; in short; in conclusion; ultimately; key takeaways; the bottom line; this section outlines; this slide shows; as discussed; below we.

Inflated vocabulary: delve; crucial; pivotal; vital; critical, key, robust, significant, strong (unless defined or statistical); landscape (figurative); navigate; foster; underscore; highlight (verb); showcase; comprehensive; streamline; enhance; facilitate; bolster; harness; empower; paramount; myriad; plethora; realm; tapestry; testament; nuanced; multifaceted; intricate; interplay; seamless; holistic; synergies; unlock; leverage (where "use" works); cutting-edge; world-class; best-in-class; game-changer; future-ready; end-to-end.

Stock phrases: in today's world; rapidly evolving landscape; at the heart of; against this backdrop; the numbers tell a story; this paints a picture; this is where things get interesting; the implications are profound; make no mistake; the reality is; in essence; only time will tell; at the end of the day; it goes without saying; needless to say; this highlights; this underscores; this serves as a reminder; speaks to; stands at a crossroads; navigating the complexities of; ensure optimal outcomes; going forward; moving forward; in order to; a number of; at this point in time; please be advised; kindly note; please do the needful.

Connective padding at sentence starts: furthermore; moreover; additionally; consequently; thus; hence; crucially. Use "and", "but", "so", or a new sentence.

Performed confidence: clearly; undoubtedly; excellent; great news; exciting; exclamation marks; emoji.

Adverbs without measurable meaning: very; truly; incredibly; highly; carefully; significantly (unless statistical). Replace with the measurement ("reviewed line by line", "three feedstock sources").

## 4. Structures: rewrite on sight

These survive word-level cleaning because they contain no banned words. They are the stronger tell.

- **Contrast reversals**: "not X but Y"; "it's not just X, it's Y"; "not only X but also Y"; "the question is not whether"; "X is not the same thing as Y"; "X is not merely a Y. It is a Z"; "X is no longer a Y". Allowed once or twice in a long document when correcting a genuine misreading; state the positive claim instead.
- **Verdict sentences**: a short sentence that judges before the evidence arrives. "Field evidence agrees." "The results are clear." "That is not a general rule." "X is the exception." Put the evidence first and let the claim follow as a clause, or cut the sentence.
- **Verdict tails**: ", which agrees with the review"; ", and it fits the model". Cut unless the agreement is itself a new fact.
- **Signpost sentences**: "Four results stand out." "Two limits matter." "Table 7 summarises..." as a standalone sentence. Use a bracketed reference instead.
- **Automatic triads** and three-beat comic escalation. Use the number of items the facts support.
- **Symmetrical antithesis as a sentence engine**; perfectly parallel paragraphs; every bullet a bold label and a colon.
- **Paragraph-end slogans**, mini TED-talk conclusions, quotables built for LinkedIn ("Strategy is what you do when pressure arrives").
- **One-line dramatic paragraphs**, the short-statement, pause, punchline rhythm, and one-line paragraphs at the end of every section.
- **Rhetorical questions as scaffolding**; batches of them to manufacture momentum.
- **Narrator commentary**: "a qualification belongs here"; "which leaves us with a better question"; "that is now part of the story"; "the useful question is".
- **Declarations of restraint**: "we make no claim about"; "this piece does not pretend to know"; "so we do not invent". State the gap as a fact and move on.
- **Reflexive caveats**: "that does not make X Y"; "it does not establish X" as a routine closing beat; "the figures differ, but the direction is clear".
- **Watch-list formula**: "X would matter more than Y"; "X matters more than the headline". Name the signal and the threshold.
- **Consequence chains**: ", so [result]" sentence after sentence; trailing ", which [verb]s"; "therefore" at every turn. When one habit is removed, check that its replacement has not become the next habit.
- **Restated findings**: the same conclusion repeated as an aphorism in the opening, the body and the close. Keep it where it is proven, with its number.
- **Contrast or verdict headings**: "What It Means", "The Path Forward", "Where It Binds", "Water Throughput, Not Pressure Ratio, Predicts...". Use a descriptive noun phrase or the analytical fact.
- **Generic openers**: "Mineral scale is one of the main constraints in X." Open with the fact and its number: "Scale cut output at the worst-affected wells by about a fifth in December."
- **Repeated skeletons**: five sentences in a row shaped "[Actor] [verb]s X". Merge them or use a table.
- **Substitutable sentences**: if another company, field or country could be pasted in without changing a word, the sentence carries nothing.

## 4a. Orwell's six rules and the Economist's tone tests

From the Economist Style Guide (2005 edition, in the house library), which prints Orwell's rules from "Politics and the English Language":

1. Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

Tone tests from the same guide: do not be stuffy; do not be hectoring or arrogant (let the analysis show that something is silly rather than calling it silly); do not be too pleased with yourself; do not be too chatty; do not be too didactic; persuade with argument and evidence and go easy on "ought" and "should". Avoid opening sentences with imperatives aimed at the reader: Compare, Consider, Expect, Imagine, Look at, Note, Prepare for, Remember, Take. Prefer the plain word: let, buy, show, about, but, use, plant.

Where the Economist and this OS differ, this OS wins: "per cent" in prose (not "%"), dates as `26 November 2026`.

## 5. Verdict, signpost and rhythm: how a human does it

A human expert starts with the point, varies sentence length because the content varies, says what he knows and what he does not without hedging every line, and stops when the point is made. A short sentence earns its place by carrying a fact ("Hot water removes halite."). A short sentence that only carries a judgement is usually a verdict.

Prefer prose for reasoning, tables for numbers, bullets for genuinely parallel items. Bold only what a skimming reader must find.

## 6. Figurative language

- **Technical rooms** (operations, consulting, bids, academic results): no metaphor, periphrasis or casual verbs. "The water holds up to 0.4 g/L of scale potential", not "carries a large reserve of scale". "Effervesced", not "fizzed".
- **Seven Gates and essays**: one figure of speech may carry a mechanism faster than the mechanism can carry itself. It must pay rent (`core/01-voice.md` section 6), it must not belong to a retired family (`core/05-memory.md`), and it must not be personification of a financial variable.
- **Never** decorate. If the sentence improves when the wit is removed, remove it.

## 8. Mechanical scan

For anything longer than about 400 words, count before and after each pass instead of trusting a read-through. Run `tools/lokoja_lint.py`; it reports these.

| Pattern | Healthy level | Action if over |
|---|---|---|
| Em dashes | 0 | Replace |
| Slop lexicon hits | 0 | Replace |
| Retired-register hits | 0 | Rewrite the construction, not just the noun |
| "X, not Y" and "not X but Y" | 0 to 2 per document | State the positive claim |
| ", so" consequence clauses | about 1.5 per 1,000 words | Split or use "Because" |
| Trailing ", which" clauses | about 3 per 1,000 words | Split or cut |
| Semicolons in prose | about 5 per 1,000 words | Split; never three in a sentence |
| Short sentences (8 words or fewer) with no number | Review each | Keep facts, cut verdicts and signposts |
| Paragraph-final sentences of 12 words or fewer | Under a third of paragraphs | Cut performative closers |
| Adjacent sentences with the same first word | Review each pair | Vary or merge |
| Repeated 4-word phrases (3 or more times) | Review each | Remove restated points |
| Sentence-length spread (standard deviation) | Above 6 words for essays | Vary deliberately by content |
| Rhetorical questions | 0 in technical rooms; 2 at most in an essay | Convert to statements |
| Triads ("A, B and C" lists) | Review each | Use the number the facts support |

Watch "which", "carries", "against", "about", "alone", "quietly", "simply", "real" and "actually" for overuse, and recount any pattern that a rewrite was used to replace.

## 9. Pass order for long documents

1. **Content**: facts, figures, units, the decision, the one thing the reader must take away. Writing cannot fix missing content.
2. **Lexicon pass**: section 3.
3. **Structure pass**: section 4.
4. **Verdict pass**: verdicts, signposts and tails.
5. **Syntax pass**: section 2, sentence by sentence.
6. **Rhythm pass**: run the scan; thin ", so", ", which" and semicolons; recount.
7. **Repetition pass**: each finding, caveat and figure stated once in the body.
8. **Freshness pass**: `core/05-memory.md`, structurally as well as lexically.
9. **Accuracy pass**: every number survived; units, arithmetic and cross-references agree; no claim was sharpened beyond its evidence by the rewrite.
9a. **Completeness pass**: spelling, typos, unfinished sentences, stub bullets, leftover placeholders and dangling cross-references. A document marked "proofread" once went out with typos and an unfinished bullet at the end of its last section; read the last page with the same care as the first.
10. **Independent read** for deep mode: a fresh reader (separate agent) who has not seen the drafting looks only for tells and unclear sentences. Check its claims against the source before applying them.

## 10. Do not over-correct

- Short factual sentences are fine.
- A genuine distinction can keep "X, not Y" when that is the clearest way to say it.
- Passive voice is normal in scientific methods sections. Convert it where the actor matters.
- Keep the author's best lines, technical terms and structure when editing someone else's text. Change only what fails a check.
- Do not flatten strong writing into beige. Clean is not the same as bland.

## 11. Scoring

Score out of 50 when asked for a diagnosis, and silently before delivering anything in deep mode. Below 40, revise.

| Dimension | Question | /10 |
|---|---|---|
| Directness | Does it state the point instead of announcing it? | |
| Clarity | Can each sentence be read once? | |
| Rhythm | Does sentence and paragraph length vary with the content? | |
| Authenticity | Would a respected expert say it this way aloud? | |
| Density | Has everything cuttable been cut, with no compression? | |

Technical rooms add the Hemingway-Oil dimensions (asset specificity, accuracy, constructability, battery limits, commercial awareness, risk judgement, decision quality) for a score out of 120 when a bid, report or paper is graded.

## 12. Worked pair

Before:

> In today's fast-paced world, it is important to note that companies must not only adapt to change, but also embrace innovation. This highlights the need for leaders to be proactive, strategic, and resilient.

After:

> Companies need leaders who decide before the market forces them to. That means funding small experiments, killing weak ones early and giving teams room to act.

Before (technical):

> Post-trip Station A train restart sequencing slipped as instrument air header leak remediation ate into the window. Data tells us around 4 mbd at risk. Recommend we calendar a review and action the valve replacement scope.

After:

> The restart of the Station A train after the trip was delayed because the instrument air leak took longer to repair than planned. Allocation data show about 4 mbd at risk. I recommend that we schedule a review and start the valve replacement.

The figures in these pairs illustrate the method. Never copy them into a document.
