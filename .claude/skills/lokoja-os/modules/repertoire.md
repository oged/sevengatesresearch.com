# The repertoire: drawing on thinkers, writers and songwriters

The house reads widely: philosophy, economics, strategy, literature, science, history and song, from Confucius to Kendrick Lamar. The repertoire exists to explain mechanisms faster and more memorably, and to place an argument in a longer conversation. It does not exist to show that we have read things.

Governing rule: **extensive repertoire, exact deployment.** Know a great deal and use only what the sentence needs.

## 1. Lens first, name second

Start from the mechanism, never from the name.

1. State the mechanism in plain words ("a founding family's cohesion decays across generations", "a price set by two small trades is not a price for size", "an incumbent defends a margin that technology is eroding").
2. Look up lenses for that mechanism in `library/thinkers.md` (search the tags).
3. Prefer the reference that explains the mechanism faster than the mechanism explains itself. A far-field reference (a song, a novel, a philosopher) is allowed when it does that better than the home-field one (an economist, an engineer).
4. Check `library/quote-bank.md` and the ledger: is this figure, quotation or image spent in the rolling window?
5. Choose the form (section 2).
6. Verify (section 4), or downgrade the form.
7. Land it in plain words within two sentences, tied to this company, field, policy or decision.
8. Record it in the ledger and, if quoted, in the quote bank.

## 2. Forms, from safest to boldest

1. **Allusion**: name the idea, not the words. "Ibn Khaldun would recognise the third generation." No copyright issue, no misquotation risk. The default.
2. **Paraphrase with attribution**: "Hayek's point was that the knowledge a planner needs is scattered among people who will never fill in his forms." Accurate to the source; flagged as paraphrase in the ledger.
3. **Short quotation**: the shortest phrase that carries the idea, verified against a primary source, integrated into the sentence's grammar.
4. **Block quotation or quotation box**: only when the words themselves are the evidence or the editor wants the text on the page; verified; brief; songs and copyright prose limited to a short excerpt, credited to the writers.
5. **Epigraph**: at most one per piece, and only if the piece then earns it.

## 3. Density modes

- **Sparse (default for essays and research)**: zero to five quotations in a long piece; at most one improbable guest per section. Zero is perfectly acceptable.
- **Seasoned (feature essays, the treatment)**: three to five quotations where the argument turns, each doing work; allusions as needed.
- **Anthology (when quotation is the point)**: a commonplace-book piece, a reading list, an essay about a thinker, a speech, a PhD literature review, a "what X would make of Y" column. Many quotations are allowed, but each is verified, attributed with its source and date, short, and followed by commentary that adds something. The piece must still have an argument; an anthology without a thesis is a scrapbook.
- **Academic**: quotations are evidence and are cited with page numbers; prefer paraphrase with citation for anything that is not the subject of close reading.

"Quote exhaustively" means: be able to draw on any of the repertoire at any moment, and in anthology mode to use a great deal of it. It never means padding a research piece with names.

## 4. Verification

- Check wording against the primary text, the transcript or the recording. A quotation website is not a source.
- Translations: name the translator for literary works (Tolstoy in the Maude translation).
- If the wording cannot be fixed, paraphrase and attribute, or say "a line usually credited to".
- Famous quotations are the most often wrong. Misattribution watchlist (do not use as written without a primary source):
  - "I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it": Evelyn Beatrice Hall describing Voltaire's attitude, not Voltaire.
  - "The only thing necessary for the triumph of evil is for good men to do nothing": no source in Burke's writings.
  - "Be the change you wish to see in the world": a compression; not found in this form in Gandhi's writings.
  - "Insanity is doing the same thing over and over and expecting different results": not found in Einstein's writings.
  - "Lies, damned lies and statistics": popularised by Mark Twain, who credited Disraeli; the origin is uncertain.
  - "Survival of the fittest": Herbert Spencer's phrase, which Darwin adopted in later editions.
  - "The ends justify the means": a summary of a reading of Machiavelli, not his sentence.
  - "In a time of universal deceit, telling the truth is a revolutionary act": not found in Orwell.
  - "Success is not final, failure is not fatal": not found in Churchill. Treat most viral Churchill lines with suspicion.
  - "When the facts change, I change my mind": attributed to Keynes on thin evidence; flag it if used.
  - "Let them eat cake": not Marie Antoinette; Rousseau records a version earlier.
  - Most motivational lines credited to Sun Tzu, Confucius, Buddha or Rumi in English. Use a scholarly translation and cite it.
- Never invent a quotation, a scene with real people speaking, or dialogue for future or hypothetical figures.

## 5. How to quote without sounding silly or lost

- Introduce the idea, not the celebrity. Not "As Aristotle famously said". Write the idea and let the name sit in the attribution.
- Banned framings: "famously", "once said", "as the saying goes", "in the immortal words of", "to quote".
- Fit the quotation into the sentence's grammar. A quotation dropped between two sentences reads like a fridge magnet.
- Do not explain the quotation at length; if it needs explaining, it is the wrong quotation.
- Do not end a section on a quotation. End on what it means here.
- Do not stack quotations. One well-placed line beats a parade of dead men and celebrities giving evidence for the defence.
- Match the room. No cultural guests in operations or consulting findings; citations, not colour, in a thesis.
- Prefer the less obvious line from a familiar source, or the familiar idea from a less obvious source, over the familiar line everyone has read.
- Do not use lyrics as decoration. A lyric earns its place when it states the mechanism or the feeling the analysis cannot state as quickly, and the editor wants it.

## 6. Relevant now and in the future

- Choose ideas that age: mechanisms (incentives, cycles, institutions, fragility, ownership, time) outlast topical memes.
- Date-stamp current references ("as the 2026 tariff round showed") so a reader in 2030 knows the context.
- Avoid references whose meaning depends on a news cycle the reader may not share.
- Re-read a piece one year later in your head: does the reference still carry the mechanism?

## 7. Not agreeing with all of them

The repertoire includes thinkers whose politics, conduct or conclusions the house rejects. Learning from a mechanism is not endorsement.

- Use contested figures for their analytical contribution, attributed plainly, never as moral authorities.
- Where a figure's association matters to the reader's trust (for example, Carl Schmitt's role as a Nazi jurist, Karl Haushofer's influence on Lebensraum doctrine, Aleksandr Dugin's Eurasianism, Kanye West's antisemitic statements in 2022), either name the association in a clause or choose another source for the same idea.
- Present opposing schools fairly when the piece takes a side: Hayek and Keynes, Mearsheimer and Nye, Friedman and Minsky, Sowell and Piketty. A steelman is cheaper than a correction.
- Do not let the repertoire tilt toward one region or century by habit. The list in `library/thinkers.md` runs from Chinese, Indian, Persian, Arab, African, Russian, Latin American and Japanese traditions to the Western canon. Use it.

## 8. Recording

Every reference used in a published piece goes in the ledger: guest, form (allusion, paraphrase, quotation), the exact wording if quoted, the source, and verification status. Quotations also go in `library/quote-bank.md`. A reference used in the rolling window is unavailable unless the new piece is specifically about it.
