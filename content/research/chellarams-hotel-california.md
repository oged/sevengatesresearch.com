It began, as these things do now, on a phone. I was checking the two Nigerian shares I own, GTCO and MTN Nigeria, in the broker app one evening, when a name I had not thought about in thirty years surfaced in the list beneath them: CHELLARAM, in capitals, with a price beside it and no movement. Chellarams. The department store. A name from the Broad Street of another Lagos. Forty minutes later I was reading a 1944 colonial file about a plot of land in Ikoyi, which is how these evenings go.

I expected a straightforward underwrite: an old trading house, some property, a share nobody trades, the kind of stock I keep a standing grievance about. Nigerian boards are full of them. Tightly held by a family or a parent, with a free float that exists on paper and a price that is really the memory of the last time two people disagreed about it. Buying one feels less like investing than like being fitted for something. Genesis had the words for it in 1974, in a song about a young man back in New York City:

> In a trap, feel a strap<br>Holding still. Pinned for kill.<br>Chances narrow that I'll make it<br>In the cushioned straight-jacket<br>Just like 22nd Street,<br>They got me by my neck and feet.

Peter Gabriel wrote that about a delinquent in Manhattan. It reads just as well about a minority shareholder in Lagos. Thinly traded, tightly held: that was the piece I meant to write, and the second half of this one still writes it. But the rabbit hole went somewhere else first. What came up was a story about immigration and the movement of capital across borders; about a family refused a house by one empire and a homeland by the end of another, which kept trading through both; about greed and resilience and survival and the plain luck of being present when the ship comes in. It starts with a name.

Somewhere in Hyderabad, in the Sindh of the nineteenth century, a family called Daryanani stopped using its name.

Nobody disowned anybody. The family simply had a patriarch called Chellaram whose trading reputation was worth more than the lineage, and in the merchant quarters of Sindh a surname was a working asset rather than an heirloom. Community historians say this happened often among the Bhaiband traders of Hyderabad: the firm traded on the founder's name, the founder's name became the firm, and within a generation the children answered to the brand. The Daryananis became the Chellarams. The usual gloss on the new name is "beloved of Ram". The commercial gloss is simpler. A bank in Yokohama or a customs officer in Lagos could not confuse it with anybody else.

That is the detail to keep in mind while reading the rest of this piece, because the name has done a great deal of travelling since. It went to Madras, where Kishinchand Chellaram set up on his own in 1915 or 1916 depending on which family account you read. It went to Yokohama in 1918, because Japanese cloth was cheaper than Lancashire's and a Sindhi trader could sit between the two. In 1923 it went to Lagos. In 1947 it survived Partition, which took the family's homeland and left the family's trade routes. In 1974, or 1977, or 1978, the Lagos company went onto the Nigerian stock exchange, and nobody can quite agree which. Today it sits on the NGX company list in capital letters, valued at ₦7.01 billion, trading two thousand shares on a quiet day.

The name was built to be recognised everywhere. The share was not built to be sold.

## The answer first

**Our view is WATCH at ₦9.70, the close of 22 October 2026.** Chellarams is a plausible asset-backed turnaround sitting inside a listed structure that is unusually hard to leave. The property on the balance sheet is worth more than every share in issue; so are the claims against the group. Base fair value is ₦7.40 to ₦8.30 a share, a range that runs from attributable book value to a conditional break-up value with a 15 per cent haircut on the property. We would get interested below ₦6.00, and even then only by buying a little at a time over months, because the stock does not trade in the quantities that make a position reversible. Above ₦11.50 the shares price in a clean separation of the loss-making subsidiaries that nobody has yet demonstrated is legally possible. Confidence is low to medium. Horizon 24 months. Review by 31 January 2027, or earlier if a material filing arrives.

The five things that would change the view are set out near the end. The rest of this piece explains why a hundred years of staying put produced a share that behaves like this.

## Madras, Yokohama, Lagos

Kishinchand Chellaram was born in 1880 into a textile family and, by the family's own telling, was working in the business by fifteen. The community he came from, the Bhaibands of Hyderabad, had a particular way of doing business. The firm's headquarters stayed in Hyderabad. The men went abroad, often for years, and ran branches in whichever port the British had opened. The French historian Claude Markovits, who wrote the first proper study of these traders, the Sindworkis, found not a diaspora of refugees but a network of outposts: Gibraltar, Cairo, Yokohama, Manila, Freetown, Lagos. A young man was posted to a branch the way a junior banker is posted to Dubai.

Malcolm Gladwell, in *Outliers*, argues that the immigrant garment trades of New York gave families something richer than money: work with autonomy, complexity and a visible link between effort and reward, which is what parents pass to children. The Sindworki branch had all three. The manager in Lagos decided what to buy, carried the exchange risk himself, financed the cloth from Madras or Yokohama and sold it before the next ship. A mistake in freight, credit or currency ate the margin before a single bale reached Ebute Metta. The lesson was that the trade belongs to whoever is present when the ship comes in.

The Nigerian operation dates its beginning to 1923. A family history describes a team sent out; a later magazine account has Kishinchand arriving by ship himself. A corporate-archive photograph places him in Lagos in 1936. The evidence supports the enterprise and his later presence. It does not supply a passenger list for 1923, and we will not invent one.

What the archive does supply is a scene from 1944, worth a moment for what it says about staying.

In February of that year a man named Jivatsing, who managed the Lagos business of K. Chellaram and Son, applied for a residential plot in Ikoyi. The colonial administration had by then stopped calling Ikoyi a European reservation, but still ran it on racial lines by other means. Officials decided that Jivatsing himself might meet the area's informal standards. The Indian employees he proposed to house with him troubled them more. He applied again in June. They ordered a Criminal Investigation Department inquiry. No crime was alleged. Both applications failed. The historian Tim Livsey found all of this in the colonial files and published it in the [*Journal of African History*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/state-urban-space-race-late-colonialism-and-segregation-at-the-ikoyi-reservation-in-lagos-nigeria/992236C5076E00EDB406C1DA79C84E61) in 2022.

A firm could supply the colony's cloth, pay its duties and employ its clerks, and still require a police inquiry to buy a house. Chellarams stayed anyway. The empire left in 1960.

## Partition, incorporation and the family that went two ways

The Nigerian company was incorporated as a private limited company on 13 August 1947. Partition came the same week. For the land-owning and shopkeeping Hindus of Sindh, 1947 was ruin. For the Sindworki houses it was something stranger: they lost Hyderabad and Karachi, the mansions and the warehouses, and kept everything that had always lived abroad. The capital was in the branches. A new Indian company was registered in Bombay the following month. The college that bears Kishinchand's name opened in Bombay in 1954, built for a community that no longer had a home town. That much is the family's and the college's own account, and we have not found reason to doubt it.

A business that had been designed around absence turned out to be designed for exile.

The next generations divided the inheritance by geography. One line, through Kishinchand's son Lokumal, moved its capital from West Africa into dry-bulk ships and property in Hong Kong. Chellaram Shipping was set up there in 1979 by Lal Chellaram; his brother Sham spent sixteen years running the Nigerian operations before joining him. The other line, through Tahilram, then Murli, then Suresh, then Aditya, stayed in Lagos. Chief S. M. Chellaram is the managing director today. His son Aditya, the chief executive, read foreign service at Georgetown, co-founded the Lagos auction house ArtHouse Contemporary with his mother Kavita in 2008 and sits on the advisory board of the Lagos Business School. Five generations, counting the founder, pieced together from company filings, the shipping group's own history and published biographies rather than any single document.

The division was not entirely tidy. In 1975 the brothers settled the family businesses into discretionary trusts, and from the 1980s the family spent the better part of twenty years in court about them. *Chellaram v Chellaram* was decided in the English Chancery Division in 1985 and again, as *No. 2*, in April 2002, with related proceedings in Bombay, Singapore and Bermuda. The disputes concerned trustees' duties over shares in businesses run by cousins outside India, allegations that trust assets were sold at an undervalue to companies the trustees controlled, and the question of whether an English court could reach trusts administered from London over companies registered in Bermuda. It could. The cases are reported at [1985] Ch 409 and [[2002] EWHC 632 (Ch)](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWHC/Ch/2002/632.html), and trust lawyers still study them. The family's own account of the quarrel is not public, and this piece does not pretend to know it.

Leo Tolstoy built *War and Peace* on the premise that a family is a longer story than any of its members. This one has a Lagos chapter, a Hong Kong chapter and a chapter in the Chancery Division, and the Lagos chapter is the only one you can buy on an exchange. That matters. The listed company is one branch's business, not the family's fortune, and the glamour of the wider name (the fleet, the Hong Kong offices, the philanthropic foundations) does not belong to a Chellarams Plc share.

## From the shop window to the factory gate

For a generation of Nigerians the Chellarams name sat beside Kingsway, UTC and Leventis. The stores sold cloth for a ceremony, a sewing machine for the cloth and the reassurance that somebody had managed to import the thing you wanted. Import controls, currency scarcity, the recessions of the 1980s and the long global decline of the department store ended that trade. Some rivals vanished. Chellarams turned towards the people who make things. Its [audited accounts](https://doclib.ngxgroup.com/Financial_NewsDocs/47334_CHELLARAMS_PLC.-_QUARTER_5_-_FINANCIAL_STATEMENT_FOR_2026_FINANCIAL_STATEMENTS_JULY_2026.pdf) for the year to 31 March 2026 break revenue of ₦25.98 billion into three lines.

The customer is now a factory manager in Agbara or Sango Ota whose line has stopped for want of a chemical or a compressor part. It is a better business than selling imported cloth to a public that can no longer afford imports, and a duller one. There is no shop window in Isolo.

The group tree is wider than the revenue lines. The accounts carry investments in associates, including Chellco Industries (textiles in Kaduna), Isolo Power Gen (gas-fired power for the industrial estate) and a stake in Devyani International Nigeria, the joint venture with India's RJ Corp that runs KFC in Nigeria. A sum-of-the-parts spreadsheet twitches at the word KFC. The audited accounts do not: the roughly ₦466 million gross carrying value of the associates is matched by an equal impairment allowance, which leaves nil. That does not prove the interests are worthless. It means nobody can put a positive number on them without evidence of ownership, legal rights and distributable earnings that the filings do not provide.

The three consolidated subsidiaries are in very different conditions. At March 2026, UTAS had standalone book equity of about ₦1.05 billion. Dynamic Industries, 77.71 per cent owned, had negative book equity of roughly ₦0.76 billion despite an operational recovery. Chellarams DMK, the dairy venture with Germany's DMK Group, 74 per cent owned, had negative standalone net assets of about ₦5.69 billion. Those are the audited standalone figures. Hold that last number. It is the hole in the hull.

## 433 people, then 143

The group reported 433 employees in the year to March 2018 and 143 in the year to March 2026. That is a fall of 67 per cent, spread evenly across every grade: management from 65 to 21, senior staff from 120 to 41, junior and supervisory staff from 248 to 81, all from the employee notes to the two sets of audited accounts. Earlier crisis-era reports referred to closures in Kano and Port Harcourt and to reduced working. The disclosures record a contraction; they do not say how many people retired, resigned, moved to contractors or were let go, and we will not guess.

Of the 143 who remain, six were paid below ₦1 million in FY2026, 42 between ₦1 million and ₦2 million, 55 between ₦2 million and ₦3 million, 18 between ₦3 million and ₦4 million, 12 between ₦4 million and ₦5 million, and ten above ₦5 million. The median employee therefore sits in the ₦2 million to ₦3 million band, which is ₦167,000 to ₦250,000 a month before deductions. Total staff and related pension costs were about ₦343 million, or ₦2.4 million per head as a blunt average, which is our arithmetic and nobody's pay slip. The company contributes 10 per cent of qualifying pay to pensions against the employee's 8 per cent and spent ₦4.25 million on education assistance. The filings say nothing useful about safety, grievances or progression. We found no basis to call Chellarams either a model employer or a bad one, and no sourced list of prominent former employees of the kind a century-old firm might be expected to have. Pleasant inventions have done enough damage in investment writing.

One item needs careful handling. A December 2023 procedural ruling of the National Industrial Court lists a matter brought by 23 claimants against Chellarams Plc and Chellarams DMK. It is evidence that a dispute exists. It is not a finding that the company withheld anything from anyone, and it should not be read as one.

## What the owners are paid, and what the owners own

According to the [June 2026 quarterly statement](https://doclib.ngxgroup.com/Financial_NewsDocs/47650_CHELLARAMS_PLC.-_QUARTER_1_-_FINANCIAL_STATEMENT_FOR_2027_FINANCIAL_STATEMENTS_JULY_2026.pdf), interests represented by Chief S. M. Chellaram, through Chellsons (Bermuda) Limited, hold 40.00 per cent of the shares. Interests represented by Aditya Chellaram, through Westfield Consultants Limited, hold 16.40 per cent. Together, 56.40 per cent. Introtex Corporation holds 19.05 per cent and the Murli T. Chellaram Foundation 6.79 per cent. We do not add those two to the family's stake, because a foundation bearing the family name is not an individual's beneficial holding and the ownership of Introtex is not documented in the filings. What can be said is that four holders account for 82.24 per cent and the company's disclosed free float is 15.01 per cent, or about 108.5 million shares.

A minority holder cannot outvote any of this: no disposal, dividend, related-party review or change of management happens without the family. He is a guest in a family house, which means reading the small print on what the hosts take.

The small print is thin. Aggregate directors' fees in FY2026 were about ₦6 million, down from ₦10.5 million. That line is not the total remuneration of the two executive directors; salaries, allowances, benefits and pensions are not bridged person by person in the filings we have read. Management described restraint on its own pay during the crisis years, which is a management claim and may well be true. It is still reasonable for a shareholder to want the reconciliation.

The 2021 episode belongs here too. The NGX [suspended trading](https://proshare.co/articles/legacy/news/59908) in Chellarams on 30 September 2021 because the audited accounts for the year to March 2021 were late, and lifted the suspension on 12 November 2021 once they were filed. A holder of this stock has once before found the quoted price unavailable to him for six weeks.

## The accounts, and where the cash went

FY2026 was the recovery year on paper. Revenue rose from ₦23.10 billion to ₦25.98 billion. Group profit before tax was about ₦0.66 billion against a loss the year before. But the year included net foreign-exchange gains of roughly ₦0.91 billion, which is more than the pre-tax profit, and profit attributable to the parent's shareholders was only about ₦0.20 billion after minorities. Finance costs were close to ₦1 billion, three times the entire wage bill. The directors recommended no dividend, for the fifth year running. (A third-party summary of the same year gives pre-tax profit of ₦0.52 billion rather than ₦0.66 billion. We use the audited figure and explain the gap in the notes.)

The [quarter to June 2026](https://doclib.ngxgroup.com/Financial_NewsDocs/47650_CHELLARAMS_PLC.-_QUARTER_1_-_FINANCIAL_STATEMENT_FOR_2027_FINANCIAL_STATEMENTS_JULY_2026.pdf) was better still: revenue of ₦9.66 billion, group profit after tax of ₦1.23 billion, ₦1.10 billion of it attributable. The cash-flow statement is quieter. Operating cash flow was about ₦373 million. Capital expenditure took ₦359 million. The difference, before a single naira of interest or principal, was ₦13.5 million. One quarter of operating cash flow does not cover one quarter of finance costs, and a profit that is mostly revaluation and translation is not a profit a creditor accepts.

Two earlier items make the trend harder to read than the headlines suggest. The FY2023 result was flattered by about ₦4.69 billion of loan-interest forgiveness, which does not recur. And the FY2026 cash-flow statement carries a large non-cash entry for the reclassification of the Isolo site from plant to investment property at ₦11.23 billion, so any comparison of cash flows across the two years has to strip it out first.

At June 2026 the balance sheet showed gross borrowings, including debt from the promoters, of roughly ₦5.1 to 5.2 billion, cash of about ₦235 million and current liabilities exceeding current assets by about ₦7.89 billion. Attributable book equity was ₦5.33 billion, or ₦7.37 a share. At ₦9.70 the market pays 1.32 times that.

## The yard that is worth more than the company

The Isolo property covers about 42,320 square metres, of which roughly 34,246 are let to third parties. It earned about ₦713.5 million of rent in FY2026. At the ₦11.23 billion carrying value that is, by our arithmetic, a gross yield of 6.35 per cent before running costs, vacancies and tax. There is no independent sale appraisal, rent roll or title opinion in the public record. A valuation in an annual report is an estimate the auditors accepted. It is not a cheque.

Still, ₦11.23 billion of property against ₦7.01 billion of market capitalisation is the fact that brings people to this stock. Begin with the easy version: hold everything else constant and change only the property value.

Now the harder version, the one the bulls prefer. Keep UTAS, which has positive equity. Walk away from Dynamic and DMK, whose equity is negative, for nil. Value what is left. On the June 2026 parent net assets of about ₦7.71 billion, plus UTAS's last audited standalone equity of ₦1.05 billion, less a ₦325 million allowance for known exposures, that comes to ₦11.66 a share with the property at its reported value and no further reserve. Haircut the property by 15 per cent and add a ₦750 million contingency, and it is about ₦8.30. Haircut the property by 30 per cent and add the reserve, and it is ₦4.93. These are our estimates, and every one of them assumes a legal separation that nobody has demonstrated.

This is where the sum of the parts meets the people who are owed money. The family changed its name once because the brand was worth more than the lineage. A subsidiary's debt does not change its name because the parent writes the shareholding down to nil. The ₦5.69 billion hole in the dairy subsidiary's balance sheet is owed to banks and suppliers who have never seen an analyst's spreadsheet and would not be moved by one. If the parent has guaranteed the debt, the hole walks upstairs. If it has lent to the subsidiary, the loan is worth what the subsidiary can repay. If it has done neither, the clean exit is real, and the shares are cheap. The filings we have do not settle it, which is why the clean-exit numbers above are labelled conditional and why the view is WATCH rather than ACCUMULATE.

Remember the yard supervisor from the illustration, who knows his tenants by their generators. He is the one who would notice first if a tenant stopped paying rent. The balance sheet would find out at the next audit.

## Welcome to the Hotel California

The Eagles released "Hotel California" at the end of 1976. Chellarams had by then been trading in Lagos for 53 years and had been a public company, if you take its own listing date of November 1974, for two. The song's promise, that a guest may settle his bill whenever he likes but will not actually be leaving, is the most precise description of this share register we have found.

Two things stand out. The price sat at exactly ₦2.24 from at least September 2021 to the end of 2022, and at exactly ₦13.20 from mid-December 2025 into January 2026. A price that does not move for months has not been tested; it is the last print. And the shape of 2024 and 2025, ₦9.70 in May 2024, ₦3.70 by November, ₦16.25 by October 2025, ₦9.70 again now, is not the shape of the operating business. It is the shape of the float.

From the end of December 2021 to 22 October 2026 the naira price rose 333 per cent, from ₦2.24 to ₦9.70. Over the same period the official exchange rate went from ₦435 to ₦1,334.72 per dollar. In dollars the share went from about half a cent to about three-quarters of a cent: a gain of 41 per cent in five years, before costs, and before the fact that no dividend was paid. Those are endpoint rates, the NAFEX close of 31 December 2021 and the NFEM close of 21 October 2026, and the result is a translation, not a total-return index.

The audited five-year summary shows no dividend from FY2022 to FY2026. Secondary records show 8 kobo in 2010, 10 kobo in 2011 and 2012 and 5 kobo in 2013, and nothing we can verify after. The company was admitted to the exchange's official list, by its own history, on 29 November 1974; the NGX profile says 1 April 1977; the company's history page also says 1978. Bonus issues in 2000, 2003, 2005 and 2009 would have to be unwound to compute a return since listing, and nobody has published an adjusted series. We are not going to manufacture a fifty-year compound return out of three listing dates and four unverified bonus ratios. A shareholder from the 1970s has a share that is still listed, which in Nigeria is not nothing, and no documented record of what it paid him.

Now the reception desk.

On 22 October 2026, 2,015 Chellarams shares changed hands. At ₦9.70 that is ₦19,546 of turnover: the price of a modest household appliance. On 21 October it was 10,680 shares. Selling runs the same arithmetic backwards, against a book that may contain no bid at all on a given afternoon.

None of this makes the business worthless. It changes what the quoted price means. ₦9.70 is the price at which 2,015 shares last moved. It is not the price at which 100,000 would. This is the cushioned straight-jacket. The cushion is the yard in Isolo. The straps are the register.

**An investor buying Chellarams in meaningful size may find that the decision to buy is far easier to execute than the decision to leave.**

## The strongest case against this view

An intelligent sceptic would put it like this. You have a listed company whose property alone is worth 1.6 times its market value, whose core subsidiary (UTAS) is solvent and growing, whose plastics business is recovering, which has just printed its best quarter in years, and which has been run by the same family for a century without going bust. The controlling family has no reason to let the shares languish forever; a disposal of the Isolo land, a dividend or an offer for the minorities would each resolve the discount in a day. Illiquidity is the price of being early. Buy a modest position, accept that you cannot sell it quickly, and wait.

That argument is coherent, and the view would move towards it on evidence. Five things: an independent valuation and rent roll for Isolo; a published schedule of guarantees and intercompany balances showing that DMK's negative equity stops at DMK; operating cash flow covering finance costs for two consecutive quarters; a director-by-director remuneration table; and a first dividend since FY2021. Any three would take us to ACCUMULATE on weakness. All five would make the base case look stingy.

What the argument cannot answer is the one hundred and fourteen trading days. A thesis that resolves "in a day" only helps a holder who can sell on that day. In this register, he cannot.

## Verdict

Kutuzov, in Tolstoy's telling, refuses to attack and tells the young Prince Andrei that there is nothing stronger than those two, patience and time; they will do it all. It is excellent advice for a family trading house. The Chellarams have followed it for a century: wait out the empire, wait out Partition, wait out the recessions and the devaluations, keep the name, keep the yard. Patience and time have been very good to the people who own 82 per cent of this company.

They are a different proposition for the person who owns a fraction of the remaining 15 per cent. His patience is involuntary. His time is measured in trading sessions.

**WATCH at ₦9.70, the 22 October 2026 close. Base fair value ₦7.40 to ₦8.30. Interest below ₦6.00, accumulated slowly. Walk away above ₦11.50. Horizon 24 months; review by 31 January 2027.** The name Daryanani was given up because the brand was more valuable than the lineage. The share has the brand. It does not yet have a way home.

An earlier working draft used ₦415, which was the 29 December 2021 rate. Different FX windows produce different results; none produces a dollar total return, because no dividends were paid.

**Listing date.** The company's own history and its 2019 annual report give 29 November 1974 as the date of admission to the exchange; the NGX company profile gives 1 April 1977; the company's history page separately gives 1978. Bonus issues in 2000, 2003, 2005 and 2009 are recorded in company historical disclosures without ratios we could verify. No since-listing return is computed.

**Family history and the Sindhi context.** The Daryanani-to-Chellaram surname change and the Bhaiband custom of trading under a patriarch's name come from a family history (shakunkimatrai.com, chapter 11) and from Saaz Aggarwal's Sahapedia essay on the Sindworkis, which cites Claude Markovits's scholarship. The "beloved of Ram" gloss is from surname reference sites and is offered as the usual reading, not a philological finding. The Amil and Bhaiband occupational split is described by community historians and is mentioned here only to explain the surname practice. The 1975 trusts and the litigation are taken from the English law reports: *Chellaram v Chellaram* [1985] Ch 409 (Scott J) and *Chellaram v Chellaram (No. 2)* [2002] EWHC 632 (Ch), Lawrence Collins J, 16 April 2002. The family's own account of the dispute is not public and is not characterised here.
